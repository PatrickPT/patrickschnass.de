    title: "AI org strategy"
    summary: "The structure, roles and operating model that move AI from isolated projects into everyday business."
    why: |
      Where AI sits in the organisation determines what it can achieve. A central lab without business ownership builds impressive demos that never get adopted. Fully decentralised teams duplicate work, reinvent platforms and create inconsistent risk. Neither scales.

      An AI org strategy answers the structural questions deliberately: which capabilities are centralised - platform, standards, governance - and which sit close to the business; which roles you need, from product owners to ML engineers; how funding and prioritisation work; and who is responsible for models once they run in production.

      The environment keeps shifting - new tools, new regulation, new talent markets. A clear operating model gives you a stable frame that can absorb that change: new capabilities plug into existing structures instead of triggering yet another reorganisation.
    value:

  - id: "ai-culture-advisory"
    title: "AI culture advisory"
    summary: "Building the trust, skills and habits that make people actually use AI - and use it well."
    why: |
      A tool nobody uses has zero return. Adoption is where many AI investments quietly fail: employees worry about their jobs, don't trust results they cannot explain, or simply don't know how AI fits into their daily work. At the same time, uncontrolled shadow use of public AI tools creates risk.

      Culture work addresses this directly. It starts with leadership setting a credible narrative - what AI is for, what it is not for and what it means for people. It continues with practical enablement: role-specific training, clear usage guidelines, champions in the business and feedback loops that let teams shape the tools they work with.

      This matters even more because AI capabilities keep changing. An organisation that has learned how to learn - that experiments safely, shares what works and adapts routines quickly - captures each new wave of capability faster than one that has to be convinced all over again. AI literacy is also an explicit expectation under the EU AI Act.
    value:
      - "Higher adoption and a real return on AI tools you already pay for"
      - "Less shadow AI and fewer uncontrolled risks"
      - "Employees who see AI as support, not as a threat"
      - "An organisation that adapts faster to every new capability"
    deliverables:
      - "Adoption and readiness assessment"
      - "Leadership narrative and communication plan"
      - "Role-based AI literacy and enablement programme"
      - "Usage guidelines and a champion network"

  - id: "eu-ai-act-conformity"
    title: "EU AI Act conformity"
    summary: "Understanding what the EU AI Act means for your AI systems - and building compliance into how you develop and operate them."
    why: |
      The EU AI Act is the first comprehensive AI regulation, and it applies both to companies that develop AI and to those that use it. It entered into force in August 2024 and its obligations are phasing in: bans on prohibited practices and AI literacy requirements already apply, obligations for general-purpose AI models followed, and requirements for high-risk systems arrive in further stages. Fines for the most serious violations can reach up to 7% of global annual turnover.

      Compliance starts with knowing what you have. Many companies lack an inventory of the AI systems in use - including AI embedded in purchased software - let alone a risk classification. From there, obligations differ widely: minimal-risk systems need little, while high-risk systems require risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight and monitoring.

      Treated as an afterthought, this becomes expensive rework shortly before launch. Built into development and operations from the start, it becomes a quality standard - and a trust advantage with customers and partners who increasingly ask how you govern AI. Because timelines and guidance are still evolving, a structured, living approach beats a one-off check. This does not replace legal advice - I work closely with your legal and compliance experts.
    value:
      - "Clarity on obligations for existing and planned AI systems"
      - "Compliance built into delivery instead of bolted on at the end"
      - "Trust with customers, partners and regulators"
      - "A governance setup that adapts as guidance and timelines evolve"
    deliverables:
      - "Inventory and risk classification of your AI systems"
      - "Gap analysis against the applicable obligations"
      - "Governance processes, documentation templates and oversight roles"
      - "Implementation roadmap aligned with your legal and compliance teams"

Technology is rarely what holds AI back - organisations are. Unclear ownership, teams that don't trust the output, skills locked away in one isolated lab and compliance questions that surface only at go-live stop more initiatives than any model limitation.

This service builds the organisational side of AI: who owns what, how teams work with AI every day, and how you stay compliant as regulation tightens. Done well, governance doesn't slow innovation down - it is what lets you move fast without losing the trust of customers, employees and regulators.
